# Chapter Ten: The Descent Decision

Merry did not wait for the archive to guide her deeper.

She had spent weeks learning the city's language of secrets, and she understood now that waiting was a luxury the buried could not afford. The Eraser would follow—it had become clear that the entity's ancient patience was no longer an asset but a liability. Two hundred years of restraint had calcified into something that looked like wisdom but was only exhaustion wearing the mask of acceptance.

She began with the maps.

The first ledger in the upper archive contained what the early holders had catalogued as "the vertical city"—not a theoretical description, but actual blueprints. Merry spent the first full day photographing pages with her phone, not digitizing them officially. The archive did not have electronic records, and she had no intention of leaving a trail that the other holders could follow too easily.

There were eight levels beneath Bellweather proper. Not dungeons, not exactly. More like institutional memory made physical. Each level served a purpose:

The first contained the sealing records—dates and names of the entities that had been contained, and the methods used. The documentation was meticulous. Meredith S. Vanier's handwriting dominated these pages, precise and accusatory. She had not simply recorded what had been done. She had judged it as she went, her margins filled with questions that would never be answered by the dead.

The second level held what the ledger called "the residual space"—areas where contained entities had left impressions in the stone itself. Not hauntings exactly. The notes were clinical. But there was an admission in the language: something in the architecture had been altered by proximity to captive consciousness. The city's bones had been changed.

Merry did not need to see the deeper levels to understand the pattern. Each layer was a choice to hide, to contain, to manage. And each choice was a choice to ignore.

She spent the second day reviewing what she already knew of the Eraser's history. The entity had not explained its own containment fully—there were gaps in Victor Vanier's diary, pages that had been removed with deliberate care, their stubs still visible in the binding. But Merry found references in older documents to something the early holders had called "the first dissolution"—a moment when collective memory had threatened to overwhelm individual identity.

The Eraser had been sealed not because it was evil. It had been sealed because it had made people remember whether they wanted to or not.

That was the real horror of the deep city, Merry realized. Not the entities, but the infrastructure of forgetting.

On the third day, she acted.

She gathered seventeen specific volumes and three loose ledgers. She selected them for a reason: they formed a narrative that the holders were not meant to notice. A story that ran parallel to the official record, written by people who had disagreed with containment but who lacked the power to prevent it. Teachers who had become archivists. Relatives who had hidden their own journals beside the official ones. A watchmaker's apprentice who had sketched the mechanisms of the seals and annotated them with commentary on their moral weight.

She did not ask the Eraser's permission.

Instead, she brought the materials to the central table and began to organize them in a way that made the hidden narrative visible. The Eraser watched from the shadows between shelves, its translucent form growing more opaque as the day progressed. By evening, it was almost solid.

"You are building an argument," it said.

"I am building a case," Merry corrected. "For why the vaults should be opened."

"The holders will not open them."

"Then they should fall."

The words surprised them both. Merry had not known she was going to say them until they were already in the air. But she did not take them back. The archive itself seemed to still around her, as though waiting to see if she meant it.

"You understand what that means," the Eraser said. It was not a question.

"If the holders fall, Bellweather falls. If Bellweather falls, the people have to decide for themselves what they want to remember. No more intermediaries. No more families managing collective grief like a business asset."

"And if they choose badly?"

Merry looked at the Eraser directly. "Then at least it will be their choice. That's the difference between you and them. They decided the city couldn't be trusted with its own truth. I'm deciding that it can."

The entity was silent for a long moment. Then it moved from the shadows and stood beside her, looking down at the documents spread across the table.

"There is a guardian on the third level," it said finally. "Not a seal. A person. The last holder who refused to forget. She has been waiting there for thirty years."

Merry had not expected that. The documents had all indicated that the lower vaults were self-maintaining, that the older seals did not require sentient custody. But even as she processed the information, she understood: there were things that could not be trusted to mechanism alone.

"What is her name?"

"Sylvia Vanier. Your great-great-aunt. She went into the deep city rather than accept what the family was becoming."

That changed things. A bloodline connection meant she could access the deeper seals without triggering certain protections. It also meant the weight of what she was about to do would settle directly on the family legacy—not as abstract violation, but as personal betrayal.

Merry had expected that this choice would require sacrifice. She had not expected it to feel so much like inheritance.

"When do we leave?" she asked.

"Now," the Eraser said. "Before the regular holders know you have been here long enough to learn what you have learned. Before the bells warn them."

Merry gathered the key and a small leather journal she had found in Meredith's diary—not a record, but a guide. Instructions for moving through the lower archive. Warnings about which seals responded to pressure and which required stillness. A map drawn on pages so thin they were almost transparent.

As she prepared to leave, she turned back to the shelves one final time.

"What happens to the archive?" she asked.

"It remains," the Eraser said. "Whether the city chooses to remember or to burn it, the knowledge will have been offered. The choice will belong to them."

Merry nodded. That was not quite reassurance. But it was close enough.

She stepped toward the passage that led downward, and the Eraser moved beside her. The air grew cooler as they descended. The sounds of the city above faded. And somewhere beneath their feet, in a chamber that had been sealed for three decades, an old woman heard the sound of footsteps and understood that her waiting was finally over.

The bells in the city above rang the hour.

But Merry no longer heard them as warnings.

She heard them as permission.

The deep city was waiting.

And she was ready to wake it.
