# Chapter Seven: The First Lesson

The first thing Merry noticed after she stepped back into Bellweather was that the city looked different.

Not larger, not smaller, not altered in any obvious way. The streets still leaned crooked under the morning haze. The bells still rang from the clock tower. Shop shutters still rattled open as vendors set out bread and fish and tea and the practical little trinkets that made a town feel lived in. Nothing was different, and that was the point.

Everything had changed.

She walked home with her hands tucked in her coat sleeves, the black key hidden in her pocket and warm against her palm even though the morning air was cold. Her thoughts were not her own, or not entirely. The chamber below the city still seemed to pulse at the edges of her mind, like an afterimage left when she closed her eyes. She could feel the Eraser's presence there, a weight under the city, waiting, patient, not hungry anymore but alive with the terrible politics of being remembered.

She did not tell Cassandra.

Not at first.

Her cousin was already awake when Merry reached the narrow house at the edge of the old quarter, the one with the cracked window and the prized family clock that never kept time and never quite stopped. Cassandra sat at the kitchen table with a cup of tea gone cold in front of her and her face arranged in the expression she used when she was trying not to be furious.

“You were gone all night,” Cassandra said, without looking up.

Merry set her coat on the back of a chair. “I know.”

“Do you?” Cassandra finally lifted her gaze. There were dark circles under her eyes, though she had not slept. “You disappeared after the bell. Mr. Gable vanished. Iho came to the house with that ridiculous bundle of gears and then looked as though someone had kicked the ground out from under him. And now you look like you’ve seen a ghost. Don’t insult me by pretending this is normal.”

Merry sat down across from her. “I saw the Eraser.”

Cassandra's face changed, not quite with surprise, but with something more brittle. “I suspected it was under the city. I did not think you would go looking for it.”

“I did not ‘go looking.’ It found me.”

A silence unfolded between them, taut as string pulled too tight.

Cassandra stared at her, then let out a breath through her nose. “And what did it say?”

Merry thought of the chamber of carved stone, the walls of sigils and old, dead stories, the creature in the center of it all with eyes like distant water. She thought of the black key in her hand, warm as a heartbeat, and the promise made in the dark beneath Bellweather.

“It said my family has been lying,” she said.

Cassandra was very still. “We are not the sort of family that lies casually.”

Merry almost laughed. “No. You just do it by omission. By inheritance. By telling the story in pieces until it becomes a myth.”

The older girl’s mouth tightened. “If you believe that, then you do not understand what was done to keep Bellweather alive.”

“And what was done?” Merry asked, too sharply. “What exactly did they do to keep Bellweather alive?”

Cassandra rose from the table. She crossed the room to the tall clock in the hall and touched one of the brass hands with two fingers, as if to steady the whole thing. “The family has always kept watch over the city. The key is not some joke or heirloom. It is a burden. The Eraser is not a beast to be set free. It is an appetite that can only be tethered to a single human mind. If that tether is broken, the memory-curse spreads. It takes things away. It takes the city apart.”

“Then why did Mr. Gable leave a note saying don’t let it see your shadow?”

Cassandra’s fingers curled around the clock hand. “Because there are some old, old warnings. Because some people are frightened by what they do not understand. Because I have spent my life learning not to ask too many questions.”

“Your family has been choosing people,” Merry said quietly. “Passing the key down. Making the burden feel natural.”

“Because it was necessary.”

Merry looked at her cousin then, really looked at her. Cassandra had always been the kind of person who seemed to stand in the center of any room without ever appearing to claim it. She had an authority that was not loud, but absolute. She was the one who stood nearest the family portraits, the one who kept the ledger of old names, the one who could silence a room with a single look. But under that composure there was a line of fear so rigid it had become a kind of armor.

“You were afraid,” Merry said.

Cassandra did not answer.

“You were afraid of what the Eraser would say.”

The clock gave a tiny, dissonant tick.

Then Cassandra turned and looked at her with a sort of helpless anger. “I am afraid of what happens if a person like you decides that power is a door and not a lock.”

Merry felt a strange, cold clarity settle over her. “Then you should be afraid.”

She went to the cellar before sunset.

Not because she was obligated to, though the weight of the key in her pocket made it feel like an obligation. But because once the note had been read and the bargain spoken, once the Eraser had said “Come every day,” Merry understood that the words were not a warning. They were an invitation.

The chamber beneath Bellweather was different in the daylight than it had been in the dark.

The carved symbols seemed older, more weathered, as if they had been waiting centuries for someone to return and read them. The air was damp and tasted faintly of iron. The city above was a thin, muffled thrum, only really noticeable when she stood still and listened. Bellweather had never felt like a place on a map. It felt like a secret wrapped in stone.

The Eraser was waiting in the center of the chamber.

It stood with its hands folded before it, the pale shape of its body more defined now than it had been in the shadows. Its face was the shape of a memory that never fully formed, all soft angles and half-caught expression. It did not look monstrous. It looked tired.

“You came,” it said.

“I said I would.”

“Most do not.”

“Most are afraid.”

The Eraser tilted its head. “Fear is a very useful tool.”

Merry stepped closer. The key in her pocket seemed suddenly very heavy. “You said you can teach me. You said it was a door.”

The Eraser nodded once. “And so it is. But not for the reason your family thinks.”

“Then tell me.”

The creature moved to the wall and, with a finger that left no mark on the stone, traced one of the symbols. It was a spiral. In the center, there was a line like a river dividing two shores. “Memory is not a library,” it said. “It is not shelves and boxes and dusty rooms kept under lock and key. It is a current. It pushes. It carries. It changes shape. People think memory is the thing they keep, but it is the thing that keeps them.”

Merry felt a little spark of understanding, not fully formed, like a word she almost knew. “Then what did you do?”

“I showed them what it really was.” The Eraser’s eyes fixed on her. “I took the false comfort of the past away. I stripped the lie from it. Do you know why the city is called Bellweather?”

“Because it is always changing.”

“Because people once believed they could predict the weather by the state of memory. The old names are full of wishful nonsense.” The Eraser smiled without humor. “No. Bellweather is named for the bell that rang each time a family was erased. It is a city built on forgetting. And forgetting is not the same as safety. It is simply a form of control.”

Merry swallowed. “My family—”

“Your family learned to seal what they were afraid of in order to survive,” the Eraser said. “They taught themselves to call it mercy. They told themselves they were protecting the city when they were really protecting their own version of it.”

“I don’t believe that,” Merry said, but her voice sounded thin even to her own ears.

The Eraser stepped closer. “Then let me show you.”

It extended one translucent hand.

Merry did not move away.

For a moment, there was nothing but the damp dark and the pulse of the city above. Then the Eraser touched her forehead.

Not pain. Not at first. A pressure, a sudden, impossible sensation, like being placed inside a room she had always known existed but had never entered. She saw flashes—an old woman with dark eyes and a ring of iron keys, standing where Merry stood, in a chamber under a city that had not yet learned to be afraid. She saw a man in a tweed coat with dust on his cuffs, his hands trembling as he poured ink into a ledger. She saw a child in a uniform with a silver gear in his pocket and a smile he did not yet allow himself to wear. She saw the shape of a family story, and beneath it, the true thing: fear, passed down like a recipe, from one generation to the next.

Then she gasped and stumbled backward.

The Eraser withdrew its hand at once. “There,” it said. “You see it now?”

Merry stood with both palms pressed against her own chest, as though to hold herself together. Her breath came uneven. “I saw my family in pieces.”

“And in pieces they are,” said the Eraser. “That is how memory works. The self is not a single, clean object. It is a collection of rooms, each one with a door. Some are open. Some are locked. Some are left alone because they are too painful to bear. It is not wrong to fear what those rooms contain. It is wrong to pretend they do not exist.”

Merry looked at the key in her hand again. It was no longer cold at all. It was warm, almost bright.

“So what do I do?” she asked.

The Eraser's expression softened, just a little. “You learn. You listen. You decide what to carry and what to release. That is the first lesson.”

Above them, the bells of Bellweather rang out once, twice, three times—time continuing, the city waking, the world turning on without any idea that a door had opened beneath its streets.

Merry lifted the key carefully in her palm.

For the first time since she had found it, the weight of it did not feel like a sentence.

It felt like a beginning.
